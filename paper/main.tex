\title{Overclocking the p-bit: Stochastic Binary Neural Network Inference
with Autocorrelated sMTJ Randomness}

\author{Manan~Gupta%
\thanks{M.~Gupta is with The Harker School, San Jose, CA, USA
(e-mail: mnn@yogins.com).}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Stochastic magnetic tunnel junctions (sMTJs) generate true random bits at
roughly $10^{6}\times$ lower energy than software pseudorandom generators,
making them attractive entropy sources for probabilistic-bit (p-bit) neural
hardware. A standard design rule requires sampling an sMTJ no faster than
about half its intrinsic fluctuation time, so that successive bits are
uncorrelated; existing systems obey this rule by construction, and its actual
cost to an application has not been measured. We treat the sampling ratio
$\rzero = \Delta t / \tauc$ as a continuous design knob and measure, in
simulation, how stochastic binary neural network (SBNN) classification
accuracy on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST degrades as devices are queried up to
$100\times$ faster than the rule allows. We find two sharply different
regimes. When device states re-equilibrate between inputs, accuracy is
almost unaffected: a single-sample classifier is provably immune to
autocorrelation, and a 32-sample classifier loses under one accuracy point at
$100\times$ overclocking. In continuous streaming operation, accuracy
collapses below $\rzero \approx 0.5$ because stale device states leak
information between consecutive inputs. Device-to-device variability does not
mitigate this collapse; correlation-aware training largely does, recovering
$+47$ points at $\rzero=0.02$ for a 1--3 point cost at nominal speed, and
extending the accuracy--throughput Pareto frontier several-fold beyond the
$2\tau$-rule ceiling. Our results turn a conservative device-level
constraint into a quantitative, workload-dependent design trade.
\end{abstract}

probabilistic computing, p-bit, stochastic magnetic tunnel junction,

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{G}{enerating} randomness is surprisingly expensive on
conventional hardware: algorithmic pseudorandom number generation at
gigabit-per-second rates costs approximately 30\,nJ per bit on CPUs and
3\,nJ per bit on GPUs~\cite{sun2026superparamagnetic}. A stochastic magnetic
tunnel junction (sMTJ)---a nanoscale magnet whose free layer flips between
two resistance states under thermal agitation---produces physically random
bits at roughly 0.03\,pJ per bit~\cite{sun2026superparamagnetic}, a gap of
about six orders of magnitude. This gap motivates probabilistic-bit (p-bit)
computing~\cite{camsari2017stochastic,kaiser2021probabilistic}, in which the
sMTJ is not a peripheral random number generator but the stochastic neuron
itself: an input current biases the device so that its time-averaged state
follows $\langle m \rangle = \tanh(I)$~\cite{camsari2017stochastic}, exactly
the activation required by stochastic binary neural networks (SBNNs) and
Boltzmann machines.

Every physical entropy source, however, has a clock. An sMTJ flips on its
own schedule, set by its N\'eel--Arrhenius fluctuation time; if it is read
faster than it flips, successive reads return copies of the same state
rather than fresh coin flips. Daniels \emph{et al.} showed that the
autocorrelation between adjacent samples vanishes once the sampling interval
exceeds roughly twice the mean dwell time~\cite{daniels2020energy}, and this
``$2\tau$ rule'' has since functioned as a hard design constraint: systems
are clocked slowly enough to satisfy it~\cite{singh2024cmos}, and randomness
quality is certified by statistical test suites that presume i.i.d.
sampling~\cite{vodenicarevic2017low,sun2026superparamagnetic}. For devices
engineered to the nanosecond regime~\cite{safranski2021nanosecond,
hayakawa2021nanosecond} the rule is cheap to obey; for the many demonstrated
devices with microsecond-to-millisecond dwell
times~\cite{borders2019integer}, it dictates the system clock.

What the rule has never been given is a price. No published work, to our
knowledge, measures what actually happens to an application---rather than to
a randomness test suite---when the rule is deliberately broken. This paper
asks that question for classification: \emph{how much faster than its
correlation time can you sample an sMTJ neuron before a stochastic binary
classifier fails, and what recovers the loss?}

We answer it in simulation with a physically grounded model: each hidden
unit is a two-state telegraph process whose escape rates are biased by its
synaptic input, reproducing the p-bit activation law exactly in its
stationary distribution while exposing the sampling ratio
$\rzero = \Delta t/\tauc$ as a continuous knob. Our contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Two regimes, not one threshold.} If device states
re-equilibrate between inputs (``reset'' operation, natural for batch
inference), classification is nearly immune to overclocking: single-sample
inference is \emph{exactly} unaffected for any $\rzero$ (we give the
argument in Sec.~\ref{sec:model}), and 32-sample majority inference loses
$<0.7$ accuracy points at $\rzero=0.02$, i.e.\ $100\times$ past the $2\tau$
rule. In continuous streaming operation, by contrast, accuracy collapses
below $\rzero\approx0.5$, driven not by staleness within one input but by
device state leaking across consecutive inputs.
\item \textbf{Variability does not help.} A natural conjecture is that
device-to-device dispersion in the energy barrier desynchronizes staleness
across neurons and softens the collapse. We find the opposite: because
dwell times are exponential in the barrier, dispersion produces a slow tail
of extra-stale devices that dominates, and accuracy strictly degrades with
barrier spread (by up to 5 points at $\sigma_\Delta=1.5\,k_BT$ in the
collapse region).
\item \textbf{Correlation-aware training recovers the streaming regime.}
Training the network while its activations are sampled from the same
correlated telegraph process flattens the failure curve: at
$\rzero=0.02$ streaming, accuracy rises from 41\% to 88\% at a cost of 1--3
points at nominal speed. With physically-accounted throughput (reset
operation pays a $\sim 2\tau$ re-equilibration wait per input), this
extends the accuracy--throughput Pareto frontier several-fold beyond the
$2\tau$-rule ceiling.
\end{itemize}

The result reframes autocorrelation from a defect to be engineered away
into a budget to be spent: batch-style workloads can already run far past
the nominal rule on existing devices, while streaming workloads define a
genuine boundary that training, not fabrication, moves.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{p-bits and sMTJ entropy sources}
A p-bit is a binary stochastic unit whose output at each query is
$m = \mathrm{sgn}\{\mathrm{rand}(-1,1) + \tanh I\}$, so that
$\langle m \rangle = \tanh(I)$~\cite{camsari2017stochastic}. Superparamagnetic
MTJs realize this physics natively: a low energy barrier
($E_b \sim 1.4\,k_BT$ for representative easy-plane
devices~\cite{sun2026superparamagnetic}) lets the free layer fluctuate
thermally, with dwell times following the N\'eel--Arrhenius law
$\tau = \tau_0 \exp(\Delta/k_BT)$, $\tau_0 \approx 10\,$ps--$1\,$ns
\cite{camsari2017stochastic}. Demonstrated fluctuation timescales span six
orders of magnitude: $2$--$5$\,ns autocorrelation times in easy-plane
devices~\cite{safranski2021nanosecond}, nanosecond random telegraph noise in
in-plane junctions~\cite{hayakawa2021nanosecond}, and
microsecond-to-millisecond dwell times in the perpendicular devices used in
the first p-bit factorization demonstration~\cite{borders2019integer}.
System-level demonstrations sample conservatively; e.g., a recent
CMOS-plus-nanomagnet prober oversamples its $\sim$20\,ms relaxation time by
$40\times$~\cite{singh2024cmos}.

\subsection{Randomness quality and neural inference}
Prior robustness studies of MTJ-based neural hardware concentrate on
\emph{static} imperfections: process-variation-induced shifts of the
activation sigmoid~\cite{wood2020modular,zand2018composable}, and
fabricated-array parameter spread with compensation via
retraining-free weight correction~\cite{zhang2025automatic}. On the
randomness side, sMTJ generators are validated against NIST statistical
suites~\cite{vodenicarevic2017low,sun2026superparamagnetic}, and a recent
study of software PRNGs found a threshold effect of generator quality on
MNIST training~\cite{prng2025quality}. Experimentally measured sMTJ
bitstreams have been used for stochastic-computing
inference~\cite{spintronicSC2025}, but with whatever correlation the
apparatus happened to have---correlation was not a controlled variable.
The \emph{temporal} axis---autocorrelation as a swept, parameterized
quantity mapped to task accuracy---is to our knowledge unexplored; existing
work treats the $2\tau$ decorrelation condition~\cite{daniels2020energy} as
a constraint to satisfy rather than a dimension to characterize. That noise
structure can have an optimal, nonzero operating point in neural systems has
precedent in computational neuroscience~\cite{timcheck2022optimal}, and
tolerance of device imperfection is a recurring theme in compute-in-memory
accelerator design~\cite{peng2020dnn}; we bring both perspectives to the
p-bit sampling question.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{The sMTJ neuron as a biased telegraph process}
Each hidden unit is a two-state fluctuator $s \in \{-1,+1\}$ with
input-modulated N\'eel--Arrhenius escape rates
\begin{equation}
k_{-\to+} = f\, e^{+I}, \qquad k_{+\to-} = f\, e^{-I},
\label{eq:rates}
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the (dimensionless) synaptic input and $f = f_0 e^{-\delta
\Delta}$ is the device attempt rate, with $\delta\Delta$ a per-device energy
barrier offset in units of $k_BT$. The stationary distribution is
\begin{equation}
P(s{=}{+}1) \;=\; \frac{k_{-\to+}}{k_{-\to+}+k_{+\to-}}
\;=\; \frac{1+\tanh I}{2} \;\equiv\; p,
\label{eq:peq}
\end{equation}
recovering the p-bit activation law exactly~\cite{camsari2017stochastic}:
the \emph{mean} behavior of every neuron is untouched by our model at every
operating point, and only the \emph{time structure} of its samples changes.
The correlation time follows from the total rate,
\begin{equation}
\tauc(I) = \frac{1}{k_{-\to+}+k_{+\to-}} = \frac{1}{2f\cosh I},
\label{eq:tauc}
\end{equation}
with the physically sensible property that strongly driven (saturated)
neurons decorrelate faster than undecided ones.

Queried at interval $\Delta t$, the process obeys the exact two-state
Markov propagator
\begin{equation}
P(s_{t+\Delta t}{=}{+}1 \mid s_t) = p + \left(\mathbb{1}[s_t{=}{+}1] - p\right)
e^{-\Delta t/\tauc},
\label{eq:propagator}
\end{equation}
which we use directly---there is no time-discretization error. The single
control knob is the \emph{sampling ratio}
\begin{equation}
\rzero \;=\; 2 f_0\, \Delta t \;=\; \Delta t / \tauc(0)\big|_{\delta\Delta=0},
\end{equation}
the query interval in units of the nominal zero-bias correlation time, so
that $\Delta t/\tauc = \rzero\, e^{-\delta\Delta} \cosh I$. The regime
$\rzero \gtrsim 2$ satisfies the $2\tau$ rule~\cite{daniels2020energy};
$\rzero \ll 1$ is deliberate overclocking. Barrier dispersion is modeled as
$\delta\Delta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\Delta^2)$, fixed per device per
chip, giving a log-normal spread of correlation times while leaving every
stationary firing probability---and hence all time-averaged
statistics---unchanged. This isolates the purely temporal effect of
variability, complementary to the static sigmoid-distortion studies of
\cite{wood2020modular,zand2018composable,zhang2025automatic}.

Model idealizations: we neglect read-disturb (the query does not perturb
the device), voltage-dependent asymmetries, $1/f$ resistance noise, and
temperature drift; the two-state approximation is standard for
superparamagnetic MTJs in the telegraph regime~\cite{safranski2021nanosecond,
hayakawa2021nanosecond}.

\subsection{Network, training, and operating modes}
We use a multilayer perceptron $784{-}256{-}128{-}10$ with stochastic
bipolar hidden activations: each hidden unit computes pre-activation $I$ and
outputs a fresh sample $s=\pm 1$ with $P(+1)$ given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:peq}
\emph{at every query, including inference}---the p-bit operating style, as
opposed to binarized networks that are deterministic at test
time~\cite{courbariaux2015binaryconnect,hubara2016binarized}. The real-valued
input layer and readout layer follow standard binarized-network practice.
Baseline networks are trained with ideal i.i.d.\ randomness using the
straight-through estimator~\cite{bengio2013estimating}, passing the gradient
of the mean activation $\tanh I$; correlation-aware networks are trained
identically except that hidden activations are sampled from the telegraph
model (Eq.~\eqref{eq:propagator}) with device state running continuously
across training minibatches.

At inference we distinguish two physically meaningful operating modes.
In \textbf{reset} mode, device states re-equilibrate between inputs
(equivalently, the hardware idles a few $\tauc$ per input); the $T$
stochastic passes used for one input remain mutually correlated through
Eq.~\eqref{eq:propagator}. In \textbf{streaming} mode the devices are never
idle: state also persists from each input to the next, modeling continuous
pipelined operation. Class scores are averaged over the $T$ passes.

\paragraph*{Why reset-mode single-shot inference is exactly immune}
For $T{=}1$ with equilibrated initial state, each neuron's single sample is
drawn from its stationary law, Eq.~\eqref{eq:peq}, regardless of $\rzero$:
autocorrelation affects only the \emph{joint} law of successive samples, not
any single marginal, and with layers evaluated once per pass there is no
second sample for it to correlate with. Overclocking therefore cannot
change $T{=}1$ reset accuracy at all---a null that doubles as a correctness
check on the simulator (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
All experiments use MNIST~\cite{lecun1998gradient} (60k/10k) with
Fashion-MNIST~\cite{xiao2017fashion} replication, full 10{,}000-image test
sets throughout. Networks train for 30 epochs with Adam ($10^{-3}$, cosine
decay), reaching $97.9\%$ ($T{=}32$, ideal randomness) on MNIST and $88.6\%$
on Fashion-MNIST. Sweeps cover $\rzero \in \{$10, 5, 2, 1, 0.5, 0.2, 0.1,
0.05, 0.02$\}$ and $T \in \{1,\dots,64\}$, each point averaged over three
independent noise seeds (and five chip seeds for variability experiments);
error bars are standard deviations. Headline findings were replicated across
five independently trained networks with agreement within $\pm 0.5$ points.
The simulator's stationary statistics and autocorrelation decay were
validated against Eqs.~\eqref{eq:peq}--\eqref{eq:propagator} by unit tests
(marginal error $<10^{-2}$; ACF decay-rate error $<0.02$ at lags 1--4).
Experiments run on CPU in hours; code, all per-run records, and exact
configurations are available from the author.

\subsection{Two regimes of failure (E1--E2)}
Fig.~\ref{fig:e2} shows the central result. In reset mode, overclocking
is almost free: at $\rzero = 0.02$---$100\times$ past the $2\tau$
rule---$T{=}32$ accuracy drops only from $97.85\%$ to $97.16\%$, and
$T{=}1$ accuracy is bit-for-bit independent of $\rzero$ ($96.91\%$
everywhere), matching the exactness argument of Sec.~\ref{sec:model} and
validating the simulator. The mechanism is benign: within-input
autocorrelation merely shrinks the effective number of independent samples
toward the $T{=}1$ floor, and that floor is high.

Streaming is qualitatively different. Accuracy collapses below
$\rzero \approx 0.5$, reaching $39.8\%$ ($T{=}1$) and $97.4\%\to41.0\%$
($T{=}32$) as $\rzero \to 0.02$. The damage is done \emph{between} inputs:
a stale device state carries the previous image's activation pattern into
the current one. Averaging helps---each $4\times$ increase in $T$ buys
roughly a $2$--$3\times$ overclocking margin---but cannot restore the
i.i.d.\ anchor. Notably, even at near-frozen $\rzero{=}0.02$ streaming
accuracy stays well above the $\sim$10\% constant-output floor: by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:tauc}, strongly driven neurons decorrelate $\cosh I$-fold
faster, so the input ``burns through'' the stale state via its most
saturated units. Fashion-MNIST reproduces the entire structure (reset:
$88.6\%\to87.3\%$ at $\rzero{=}0.02$; streaming: collapse to $40.8\%$),
indicating the phenomenon is not dataset-specific.

\subsection{Device variability does not desynchronize the damage (E3)}
Since dwell time is exponential in the barrier, one might hope dispersion
$\sigma_\Delta$ spreads correlation times so that at any moment some
neurons are fresh---a stochastic-resonance-like rescue with precedent in
balanced spiking networks~\cite{timcheck2022optimal}. Fig.~\ref{fig:e3}
refutes this for our setting: at fixed $\rzero$, streaming accuracy is
monotonically worse with increasing spread ($78.5\%\to73.5\%$ at
$\rzero{=}0.05$ as $\sigma_\Delta: 0\to1.5\,k_BT$), while reset mode is
indifferent. The asymmetry explains it: a fast-tail device cannot beat
``fresh''---there is no headroom above i.i.d.---while a slow-tail device
($\tau$ larger by $e^{+\delta\Delta}$) becomes catastrophically stale, so
the log-normal slow tail dominates the network-level effect. For
fabrication, this inverts the usual reading of barrier uniformity
requirements~\cite{zhang2025automatic}: uniformity matters \emph{more}, not
less, when a system will be overclocked.

\subsection{Correlation-aware training (E4)}
Training with the telegraph source active (state running continuously
across minibatches) produces networks whose streaming robustness curves are
nearly flat (Fig.~\ref{fig:e4}). The network trained at $\rzero{=}0.2$
holds $87.6\%$ at $\rzero{=}0.02$ where the ideal-trained baseline falls to
$41.0\%$; the $\rzero{=}0.5$-trained network is the better choice for
moderate overclocking ($94.1\%$ vs.\ $78.6\%$ at $\rzero{=}0.05$) at only
$1.1$ points below baseline at nominal speed. The trade is the familiar
robustness--accuracy exchange of noisy training, here induced by a
\emph{physical} noise process rather than an artificial regularizer: the
network learns activation patterns that remain decodable when a fraction of
each layer's units echo their previous state.

\label{fig:pareto}

\subsection{The throughput Pareto frontier (E5)}
Throughput accounting exposes the design consequence. Reset operation must
idle $\sim 2\tauc$ per input to re-equilibrate, so its throughput saturates
at $0.5$ images/$\tauc$ no matter how aggressively the $T$ passes are
overclocked; streaming pays no idle time but suffers the collapse; and
correlation-aware training moves the streaming curve
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto}). At a 97\% accuracy target the frontier is reset
operation with its averaging passes overclocked ($T{=}16$ at
$\rzero{=}0.02$: $97.1\%$ at $0.43$ images/$\tauc$), $1.7\times$ past the
best rule-compliant operating point; at 95\%, correlation-aware
\emph{single-shot} streaming at $\rzero{=}1$ ($95.9\%$, vs.\ $94.0\%$
ideal-trained) doubles rule-compliant throughput; and at 90\%,
correlation-aware single-shot streaming at $\rzero{=}0.5$ ($93.4\%$)
reaches $2$ images/$\tauc$, a $4.0\times$ gain.

Concretely, for the millisecond-class devices of the first p-bit
demonstrations~\cite{borders2019integer}, the $2\tau$ rule limits a
$\tauc \sim 10$\,ms device to $\sim 50$ classifications/s at $\geq 95\%$
accuracy; the frontier in Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto} makes 100
classifications/s at $\geq 95\%$, or 200/s at $\geq 90\%$, available from
the same silicon. For nanosecond easy-plane devices~\cite{safranski2021nanosecond}
the same ratios apply to systems that are sampling-rate--limited rather
than device-limited.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{Design guidance.} The $2\tau$ rule is the right specification for
randomness-certification workloads (TRNG bitstreams, NIST
compliance~\cite{vodenicarevic2017low,sun2026superparamagnetic}), but for
p-bit \emph{inference} it is workload-dependent: (i) batch-style inference
with per-input equilibration can overclock essentially without limit---the
marginal-distribution argument guarantees it for $T{=}1$ and our sweeps
bound the loss for $T\le 64$; (ii) streaming inference obeys a genuine
boundary near $\rzero \approx 0.5$, which fabrication (barrier uniformity)
does not move but training does; (iii) if a system will be overclocked,
device uniformity should be prioritized over device speed, since the slow
tail of the dwell-time distribution, not the mean, sets the damage.

\textbf{Limitations.} This is a simulation study with an idealized
two-state telegraph model; read-disturb, retention drift, and $1/f$
components in real devices~\cite{sun2026superparamagnetic} may tighten the
boundaries reported here, and our networks are small MLPs on standard image
benchmarks. The streaming results depend on input order only through the
generic assumption that consecutive inputs are uncorrelated; correlated
input streams (video) could interact with device staleness in either
direction. Validating the $\rzero$ sweep on fabricated arrays---e.g.\ the
instrumented systems of \cite{singh2024cmos}---is the natural next step, as
is extending to convolutional SBNNs and to temperature-induced bias drift,
which we deliberately excluded as a separately characterized
axis~\cite{sun2026superparamagnetic}.

\section{Conclusion}
We measured the application-level price of violating the sMTJ sampling
rule and found it is not a wall but a fork: nearly free for equilibrated
batch inference, a hard collapse for streaming inference---caused by
cross-input state leakage, immune to device-variability remedies, and
largely repaired by correlation-aware training. Treating the sampling ratio
as a design variable rather than a constraint converts device
autocorrelation into usable throughput: $2\times$ at matched 95\% accuracy
and $4\times$ at 90\%, beyond the $2\tau$-rule ceiling, from training
alone. We hope this reframing---and the
accuracy-vs-$\rzero$ curves as a device-to-application specification
chart---is useful to both device and architecture communities.
